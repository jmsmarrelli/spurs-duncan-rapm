Method notes

The reconstruction, the two things that had to be added before the ratings made sense, and what the team-versus-player bracket does and does not establish.

Rebuilding the lineups

The feed records substitutions and never records who is on the floor. The walk is the obvious one — start each period with five, apply every substitution in order, carry the set forward — and the difficulty is entirely in the failures.

Period-opening fives are the hard part. The box score marks the starters, which gives period one. For periods two onward the five is inferred from the first events of the period and the substitutions around them, and when that inference is inconsistent the game is dropped.

The check is arithmetic and external: total each player's seconds on the floor and compare with the minutes in the box score. A game is kept only if the mean absolute error across its players is under 1.5 minutes and no single player is off by more than 6. 14,332 of 15,611 games pass, at a mean error of 0.35 minutes.

output/reconstruction_audit.csv gives the count and the reason for every season. Almost all failures are lineup — a substitution that leaves six players on the floor or five who cannot be reconciled — and they concentrate in the first three seasons, where the feed is worse.

Possessions, counted rather than estimated

The usual FGA + 0.44×FTA − OREB + TOV is a decent season-level approximation and a poor stint-level one, because the 0.44 is a league-average guess about which free throws end a trip and stints are short enough for the guess to matter.

Since the log is right there, possessions are counted from it: mark every event that unambiguously identifies which team has the ball, compress the marks into runs, and call each run a possession. Ambiguous events — fouls, timeouts, jump balls, substitutions — are skipped rather than guessed at, which is safe, because dropping a marker can only merge two possessions that were already adjacent and identical. And-ones fall out correctly: the made shot and the free throw are marked to the same team, are adjacent, and collapse into the one possession they were.

Two things that had to be in the model

Both were absent from the first version of this, and both changed the answer enough to be worth naming.

An intercept. Without one, a penalized coefficient is pulled toward zero points per 100, not toward league average, and the player block has to carry the league's scoring level. Every coefficient inflates to cover it.

An unpenalized effect per season. Thirteen seasons of a league whose scoring level moved by several points per 100. Without season effects that movement also lands in the player coefficients, and players whose careers sit in high-scoring years are systematically mis-rated.

The first version I ran had Nick Calathes third in defensive RAPM on 7,400 possessions and Bradley Beal fifth, and I spent an hour looking for a bug in the lineup reconstruction before finding it in the design matrix. With the intercept and the season effects in, the top of the same table is Garnett, Bogut, Duncan, Ben Wallace and Tony Allen, and output/floor_sensitivity.csv shows the ranking barely moves as the possession floor goes from ten thousand to a hundred thousand. That stability is the evidence that the fix was a fix and not a preference.

The penalty

Five-fold cross-validation with folds split by game, on the middle three seasons of the sample. It lands at 3,000 and the curve is shallow: 2,000 and 4,000 are within 0.5 of it on a held-out error of 3,581. The same penalty is then used for every window, which is what makes the career arc comparable season to season — re-tuning per window would mix a change in the player with a change in the instrument.

The scale of a RAPM rating is set by the penalty, so a rating is only meaningful against the distribution it came from. That is why figure 03 draws the percentile band and why the leaderboards quote ranks as well as values.

Team against player: what the bracket is for

Adding a defensive effect per team-season is easy. Interpreting it is not, because a team-season effect is nearly collinear with the defensive coefficients of the players who filled that roster. The split between them is decided by which block carries the ridge penalty, not by the data.

So two things are reported, and only one of them is an estimate.

The test. Does the team block improve held-out prediction? Folds by game, same as everywhere else. This is a real question with a real answer, and the answer is 0.79 of 3,650 — consistent in sign across five folds, and two hundredths of one per cent.

The bracket. With the team block removed, shrunk exactly as hard as a player, and effectively free, the same player's rating is read off all three. The two ends bound the answer and nothing in between is estimable. For Duncan the bracket is +4.59 to +4.28; for Roy Hibbert it is +2.07 to +0.34.

A note on "effectively free": the team dummies sum to the intercept, so with no penalty at all the normal equations are exactly singular. The free convention uses a token multiplier of 1e-6 on the same ridge, which is what unpenalized means in practice.

partial_ridge exists because scikit-learn's Ridge penalises every column identically, which is precisely the thing this model must not do. At a few thousand columns the normal equations are cheap to form and solve directly.

Where this is weakest

The first six seasons of the career are not here, and they are the ones with the two Defensive Player of the Year awards in them. The mirror cannot see before 2003-04.

The early seasons that are here are the thinnest. 76% coverage in 2003-04 to 2005-06 against 96% afterwards. Games fail for reasons unrelated to who was playing well, so this is noise rather than bias, but the peak of the career is measured with a quarter less evidence than the decline.

No intervals. Ridge coefficients do not come with honest standard errors, and a bootstrap over 762,000 stints was out of budget for this project. The percentile band and the floor sensitivity are what stand in for them, and neither is a substitute. Nothing here should be read to a third decimal place.

A marginal contribution is not an ability. Figure 03 is the illustration and it is not a solvable problem: when Kawhi Leonard becomes the Defensive Player of the Year beside a 38-year-old Duncan, Duncan's marginal contribution falls because the counterfactual improved. Any rating built on on-court/off-court variation has this property.

Thirteen seasons pooled into one coefficient means the career leaderboard mixes a player's peak and decline. It is the right object for "who was the best defender of the era" and the wrong one for "who was the best defender", and the rolling windows are there for the second question.

Regular season only. The postseason is a different sample against different opposition and there is not enough of it to fit this model on.
